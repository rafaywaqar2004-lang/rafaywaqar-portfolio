1. Purpose

Every piece published on The Crescent Brief and its companion trackers (the MENASA Risk Monitor, the Gulf AI & Tech-Bloc Alignment Tracker) makes judgments about what's likely to happen next. Most published risk analysis never comes back to check those judgments against what actually happened. This page exists to close that loop: specific, dated, falsifiable forecasts, logged before the outcome is known, scored against reality once it resolves.

This is the same discipline geopolitical risk consultancies use internally to evaluate analyst performance, applied here in the open.

3. Methodology

Each forecast is logged with a probability between 0 and 100% that the stated claim resolves "Yes" by the given date. When the resolution date passes, the outcome is recorded as Yes or No based on public, citable evidence, and the forecast is scored using the Brier score, the standard method for grading probabilistic forecasts:

Brier score = (forecast probability − outcome)², where outcome is 1 if the event happened and 0 if it didn't, averaged across all resolved forecasts.

A lower Brier score is better. A score of 0 means perfect calibration; 0.25 is what a forecaster gets by guessing 50% on everything; a well-calibrated forecaster who says 70% on things that happen 70% of the time scores well below that. This is the same scoring convention used in forecasting-tournament research (Tetlock's Good Judgment Project) and by professional forecasting platforms.

  • Forecasts are never edited or deleted after logging, including their stated probability. Only the resolution outcome and date are added once known.
  • A forecast that resolves ambiguously is marked as such and excluded from the Brier calculation, with the ambiguity explained, rather than scored in either direction.
  • New forecasts are added as new pieces publish on The Crescent Brief and its companion trackers, drawn from each piece's own stated Outlook or Indicators-to-Watch section.

4. Limitations

  • Sample size is currently small. A handful of resolved forecasts is not enough to draw strong conclusions about calibration; this page discloses that plainly rather than presenting an early Brier score as more meaningful than it is.
  • Resolution judgment is the author's own, applied against publicly available evidence and explained in the resolution note once a forecast closes; it is not adjudicated by an independent third party.
  • This is a research and portfolio exercise, not a commissioned forecasting product, and should be read accordingly.
