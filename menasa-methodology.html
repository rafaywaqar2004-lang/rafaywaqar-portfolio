MENASA Sovereign Risk Monitor — Methodology

A transparent framework for monitoring macroeconomic, governance, and political-economic vulnerability across MENA, South Asia, and the Horn of Africa.
Open Live Monitor Return to Portfolio View GitHub

1. Purpose

The MENASA Sovereign Risk Monitor is designed as a structured country-risk screening and monitoring tool. It helps users identify changes in macroeconomic and governance vulnerability, compare country profiles, and prioritize areas for deeper analyst review. It does not forecast crises with certainty and should not be used as investment advice or as a substitute for country-specific due diligence.

2. Coverage

  • 34 economies across MENA, South Asia, and the Horn of Africa
  • Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, UAE, Yemen, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia, Somalia, Djibouti, and Eritrea
  • Historical coverage from 2010 to present
  • Country-level monitoring and comparisons

3. Framework

The monitor combines 10 macroeconomic and governance indicators across two pillars — economic and governance — weighted equally at 10% by default, with weights adjustable live in the app's Scenario Explorer. The quantitative score is built primarily on the World Bank's public API (WDI and WGI). IMF World Economic Outlook data is used as an automatic fallback for debt-to-GDP figures on countries the World Bank does not report consistently. Indicators are normalized and combined into a composite framework designed to make cross-country comparison transparent and interpretable.

4. Weighting and PCA

Principal component analysis is used as a diagnostic check for factor redundancy and correlation structure. It should not be interpreted as proof that the framework predicts sovereign crises. The monitor is designed to support transparent analytical judgment rather than replace it with a single automated output.

5. Missing Data

Where country data are unavailable or constrained, the monitor documents the limitation and applies transparent handling rules rather than concealing missingness — a country's missing factors render as a visible "not reported" marker on the radar chart rather than silently shrinking the shape, and no value is invented to fill a gap. Debt-to-GDP coverage is a known gap area: the World Bank does not report it consistently for all 34 countries, so the IMF's World Economic Outlook is used as an automatic fallback. Iran's composite score is lower-confidence, with fewer of the 10 factors available, likely due to sanctions limiting fiscal data reporting. Users should interpret scores alongside data-availability notes and country-specific context.

6. Historical Validation

The project includes historical comparison against documented regional crises to assess whether the composite framework moved in a direction consistent with observed deterioration. Results include same-year captures, lagged captures, and misses. This exercise is intended as a diagnostic assessment of monitoring usefulness, not a claim of deterministic prediction.

7. Limitations

  • Public-source data can be revised, delayed, incomplete, or inconsistently reported.
  • Default weights (10% per factor) are a transparent, reasonable starting point — not a backtested or econometrically validated model.
  • Composite scores simplify complex country conditions and cannot capture all political or institutional nuance.
  • Cross-country comparisons should be interpreted alongside qualitative research.
  • Risk indicators can identify vulnerability but cannot determine the timing or certainty of specific events.
  • The monitor is not investment advice.

8. Version and Updates

Data pipeline: automated weekly refresh via GitHub Actions (every Monday) — re-pulls World Bank and IMF data, recomputes scores, validates every dataset, and redeploys
