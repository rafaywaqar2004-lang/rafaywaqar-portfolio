MENASA Sovereign Risk Monitor — Methodology

A transparent framework for monitoring macroeconomic, governance, and political-economic vulnerability across MENA, South Asia, and the Horn of Africa.

MENASA Sovereign Risk Monitor is a live country-risk platform covering 34 economies across MENA, South Asia, and the Horn of Africa. It integrates 11 macroeconomic and governance indicators across two equally-weighted pillars — reported both as one composite score and as separate Economic Risk Index / Political Risk Index sub-scores — built primarily on World Bank data (with IMF data used as a fallback for debt coverage), helping users identify where sovereign and political-economic vulnerabilities are rising, why they are changing, and how alternative assumptions affect the outlook. A sourced Upcoming Catalysts calendar of confirmed, dated near-term events (elections, IMF program reviews) complements the backward-looking composite with a forward-looking layer that is never estimated or fabricated. A separate Drivers Analysis tab runs Pooled OLS / Fixed Effects / Random Effects panel regressions with clustered standard errors and a Hausman test on the same underlying data, with one-click R and Stata code export for independent reproduction. Designed as a research and monitoring tool; not investment advice.

1. Purpose

The MENASA Sovereign Risk Monitor is designed as a structured country-risk screening and monitoring tool. It helps users identify changes in macroeconomic and governance vulnerability, compare country profiles, and prioritize areas for deeper analyst review. It does not forecast crises with certainty and should not be used as investment advice or as a substitute for country-specific due diligence.

2. Coverage

  • 34 economies across MENA, South Asia, and the Horn of Africa
  • Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, UAE, Yemen, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia, Somalia, Djibouti, and Eritrea
  • Historical coverage from 2010 to present
  • Country-level monitoring and comparisons

Every score is a relative ranking within this 34-country sample, not an absolute, globally-benchmarked index. Each factor is min-max normalized against only the other 33 tracked MENASA economies for that year, not the full ~190-country UN membership. A "Lower Risk" score here means lower risk relative to this specific regional pool — it does not mean the country would also rank as low-risk against, say, Western Europe or East Asia. This matters when comparing scores or tiers to any country outside this 34-country set, including via the app's own credit-rating comparison, which draws on actual global agency ratings.

3. Framework

Each country is scored 0–100 (100 = highest risk) on a weighted composite of 11 factors across two pillars, each pillar weighted equally at 50% by default — economic factors at roughly 8.3% each (1/12 of the composite), governance factors at 10% each — adjustable live in the app's Scenario Explorer. All 11 factors are pulled from the World Bank's public API (WDI = World Development Indicators, WGI = Worldwide Governance Indicators), the same underlying database the IMF, credit rating agencies, and academic researchers use as a baseline, refreshed weekly via an automated GitHub Actions pipeline.

4. Economic Risk Index & Political Risk Index

The two pillars above are also reported on their own, as separate 0–100 sub-scores — not a second model, just the composite's existing halves shown individually rather than only blended together. Because the Economic pillar (debt, current account, reserves, GDP growth, inflation, currency depreciation) and the Governance/Political pillar (political stability, government effectiveness, rule of law, regulatory quality, control of corruption) are each exactly 50% of the composite's weight, the relationship is exact: composite score = 0.5 × Economic Risk Index + 0.5 × Political Risk Index whenever both pillars have full factor coverage for that country.

The point is to stop a single blended number from hiding two very different risk profiles. A country can carry low economic risk but comparatively high political risk (several Gulf states pair strong fiscal buffers with a low composite score that still masks meaningfully elevated governance-risk sub-scores), or the reverse — fiscally strained but comparatively stably governed. The app surfaces this on every Country Deep Dive page (both sub-scores shown side by side) and as a region-wide Economic Risk vs. Political Risk quadrant chart on the Overview tab, plotting all 34 countries by their two sub-scores at once.

6. Weighting and Validation

Pillar-balanced weighting (50% economic, 50% governance, equal within each pillar) is a deliberate starting point, not a claim that all 11 factors matter equally in reality. Two independent checks were run against this same historical data: a redundancy check (principal component analysis across all 11 factors, to test for multicollinearity) and a predictive-validity check (backtesting the composite's year-over-year direction against six real, documented crises — see Historical Validation below).

The redundancy check ran on the 77 of 507 country-year observations in the underlying dataset that report all 11 factors simultaneously (a stricter complete-case approach, chosen over a pairwise-correlation matrix given how unevenly coverage varies factor to factor). The first principal component explains 41% of total variance, and it is dominated by the governance pillar — Rule of Law, Control of Corruption, Political Stability, and Government Effectiveness all load onto it in the same direction with similar strength (loadings of 0.38–0.45), while Regulatory Quality is comparatively less redundant. Concretely: Rule of Law correlates 0.84 with Control of Corruption and 0.81 with Political Stability, and Government Effectiveness correlates 0.76 with Control of Corruption — four of the five governance factors are substantially capturing the same underlying signal. The economic factors tell the opposite story: GDP Growth, Inflation, and Currency Depreciation load weakly on that dominant governance component and separate onto their own axis (a second component, 17% of variance, driven mostly by Current Account, Debt, Inflation, and Currency Depreciation moving together) — meaning Currency Depreciation is adding real, distinct signal rather than restating what governance already captures.

This confirms real redundancy inside the governance pillar — closer to 2–3 independent dimensions than 5 — but the composite still uses equal weighting within each pillar deliberately: down-weighting the correlated factors based on this same historical sample would mean fitting the model to the data it was just validated against, the exact overfitting risk this check is meant to guard against. A "PCA-Derived (data-driven, experimental)" preset built from these exact loadings is available as one additional, optional lens in the app's Shock Scenario Lab (Weight Sensitivity tool) — never a replacement for the default weighting anywhere else, and carrying the same overfitting caveat there.

A further, market-implied validation step — correlating the score against CDS spreads or bond yields — was deliberately not attempted: true backtesting needs point-in-time data vintages (the score as it would have looked at the time, not recomputed with data revised since) that the World Bank's API doesn't expose, and reliable market pricing doesn't exist for most of these 34 economies. That stays an explicit, disclosed gap rather than a papered-over one.

7. Missing Data

Where country data are unavailable, the monitor documents the limitation and applies transparent handling rules rather than concealing missingness — a country's missing factor renders as a visible "not reported" marker on its radar chart rather than silently shrinking the shape, and no value is ever invented to fill a gap. If a country is missing a factor, that factor is dropped and the remaining weights are rescaled proportionally. Debt-to-GDP coverage is a known gap area: 17 of the 34 countries have no debt-to-GDP figure on file directly from the World Bank, so IMF World Economic Outlook data is used as an automatic fallback — even after that fallback, only 31 of 34 report it consistently. Iran's composite score is lower-confidence, with only 9 of the 11 factors available (Current Account and Reserves are both missing), likely due to sanctions limiting fiscal data reporting.

8. Historical Validation

Six well-documented, unambiguous crises were checked against the project's own historical scores (2010–present), with no cherry-picking of only the successes. This tests direction and timing only — did the score move in the right year, not whether the exact magnitude is calibrated.

  • Syria — civil war escalation: +22.0 points in a single year (2011→2012), the sharpest same-year capture of any case tested.
  • Sri Lanka — 2022 economic collapse & default: +13.9 in 2022; the recovery is captured too, -16.2 in 2023 as the IMF program took hold.
  • Afghanistan — 2021 Taliban takeover: +14.2 in the same calendar year — a same-year capture of a sudden political shock.
  • Lebanon — 2019–2023 financial collapse: essentially flat in 2019 (the actual onset), then climbs every year 2020–2023 — a real, sustained, correctly-directional capture, but roughly a one-year lag.
  • Pakistan — 2022–2023 balance-of-payments crisis: +3.4 in 2022 itself (after Currency Depreciation was added as a factor), then -2.9 in 2023 and -1.3 in 2024 as the IMF Stand-By Arrangement took hold.
  • Egypt — 2022–2023 currency crisis: still a miss. Currency Depreciation was added specifically because this case didn't move the prior 10-factor score at all; it helps partially (Egypt's normalized currency-risk reading rises to 22.6 in 2022), but the composite still falls slightly both years — partly because Lebanon's currency depreciated over 800% the same year, which compresses every other country's currency-risk reading on the same relative 0–100 scale.

Overall: 4 of 6 cases captured in the same calendar year (Syria, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Pakistan), 1 captured correctly but roughly a year late (Lebanon), and 1 still substantially missed (Egypt) — an honest scorecard, not a claim of a perfect early-warning system.

9. Upcoming Catalysts

The composite score is annual and backward-looking by construction (see Historical Validation above). Upcoming Catalysts is a small, deliberately incomplete calendar layered on top of it: concrete, dated, forward-looking events — scheduled elections, IMF program review windows, and similar institutional milestones — shown per country and as a region-wide chronological table on the Overview tab.

The sourcing discipline is the same one this project applies everywhere else: every entry is verified against an official or primary source — an IMF press release or program document, a national election authority, or a major wire service — never estimated or projected. A country with nothing verified shows an explicit "no confirmed near-term catalyst identified" message rather than a manufactured placeholder, the same "never silently fill a gap" principle already applied to missing factor data (see Missing Data above).

As of this update, 12 of the 34 countries carry a confirmed, sourced entry — among them Morocco's September 2026 general election, Israel's October 2026 election, South Sudan's first general election since independence (December 2026), and IMF program review windows for Egypt, Jordan, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh. The other 22 countries carry no entry, which reflects the discipline of the sourcing bar, not an absence of political or fiscal risk.

10. Drivers Analysis (Panel Regressions)

A separate "Drivers Analysis" tab runs panel regressions on this project's own 34-country, 2010–present dataset to test which macroeconomic and governance variables statistically predict political stability — Pooled OLS, Fixed Effects (entity + time, standard errors clustered by country), and Random Effects, with a Hausman test comparing the latter two. Users can toggle which of 7 predictors (debt, GDP growth, inflation, current account, reserves cover, trade openness, government expenditure) enter the model, with a live coverage table showing exactly how many country-years survive listwise deletion for the current selection — several series (debt-to-GDP and government expenditure especially) have genuinely sparse historical reporting for these 34 economies, disclosed directly rather than hidden.

Three robustness checks run alongside the main results: predictors lagged one year, leave-one-country-out re-estimation (to catch a single country silently driving a coefficient), and an ACLED-based conflict-onset alternative dependent variable — the last of which stays visibly disabled with an explanatory message in this deployment rather than approximated with fabricated conflict data, since it requires a registered ACLED API key this deployment doesn't hold.

Built for independent verification, not just display: a "Download R Code" and "Download Stata Code" button each export a complete reproduction script (plm in R, xtreg in Stata) matching whatever predictor selection is currently active in the app, alongside a "Download Panel Data (CSV)" button for the underlying data those scripts run against.

Explicitly scoped as illustrative, academic-style analysis, not a publication-quality causal study — the in-app Methodology section for this tab covers the same standard caveats as any panel study of this kind: endogeneity/reverse causality between debt and instability, omitted-variable bias, a short within-country time dimension, and the WGI dependent variable's own annual, backward-looking, estimated (not precisely measured) nature.

11. Shock Scenario Lab

The former "Scenario Explorer" tab is upgraded into a Shock Scenario Lab: four calibrated shock scenarios — a Strait of Hormuz closure, a Red Sea/Bab-el-Mandeb disruption, a Pakistan sovereign default, and a fully custom shock builder — that flow oil-price, shipping-cost, conflict, and currency shocks through disclosed transmission formulas (energy fiscal exposure, freight-cost pass-through to import prices, reserve-cover depletion, and conflict/currency point adjustments) into a per-country point delta on the existing composite score.

Every calibration constant behind these formulas — chokepoint trade-exposure classifications, the share of a country's imports assumed to be energy versus freight-cost-sensitive, points-per-percent conversion factors — is explicitly disclosed as a modeling assumption, not a measured statistic, in a "Transmission Model & Assumptions" write-up inside the tab itself. Four visualizations accompany the results: a before/after ranked bar chart, an exposure-vs-impact scatter, an impact choropleth map, and a sector/channel impact heatmap, alongside a full results table.

The original weight-reweighting/normalization-sensitivity tool from the prior Scenario Explorer is preserved unchanged, nested as a "Weight Sensitivity" sub-section within the new tab — nothing about the original functionality was removed. Every scenario carries the same explicit disclaimer: "These scenarios use simplified transmission assumptions for analytical illustration. They are not forecasts."

12. Market Signals

A "Market Signals" sub-section on every Country Deep Dive page adds four supplementary, market-based indicators below the existing World Bank/WGI risk scores — additive context only, never part of the composite score or any factor weight. The Exchange Rate Pressure Index now runs on real DAILY data where available: a daily GitHub Action fetches the free, no-key ExchangeRate-API "Open Access" endpoint once every 24 hours and accumulates one real snapshot per country into its own history file — the only honest way to build a historical series from a free API that only ever returns today's rate. Until roughly nine real daily snapshots exist for a given country, the signal transparently falls back to the project's original real annual World Bank exchange-rate calculation (IMF's monthly effective-exchange-rate API was evaluated live and found unworkable — its real indicator codes are undocumented composite strings that returned empty datasets even for reference queries). The two granularities are never blended; the app always discloses which one produced the figure shown.

Live ACLED conflict-event counts (30/90/365-day windows with a simple trend flag) and a live UN Comtrade import-partner concentration index (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) round out three of the four signals. The deployed app carries its own default UN Comtrade key so real trade-concentration data shows for every visitor by default (ACLED access is pending the same treatment, subject to their approval process); a "Market Data Settings" sidebar panel also lets any visitor use their own account for either source instead, kept in browser session state only and never written to disk. The fourth, Sovereign Bond Yield, is honestly always reported as "N/A": a full scan of the World Bank WDI indicator catalog and IMF's public APIs turned up no free source for market sovereign bond yields across this country set, so the real data gap is disclosed rather than papered over with a mislabeled proxy interest rate.

Consistent with the project's "never fabricate to fill a gap" discipline: with no API keys configured, the app works exactly as before and every affected row simply reads "Not configured" — and a failed request (an invalid key, an unreachable API) is distinguished from a genuine zero-result response rather than showing the same thing for both.

13. Limitations

  • Public-source data can be revised, delayed, incomplete, or inconsistently reported.
  • Default weights (pillar-balanced, equal within each pillar) are a transparent starting point — not a backtested or econometrically validated model (see Weighting and Validation above for what was and wasn't tested).
  • Composite scores simplify complex country conditions and cannot capture all political or institutional nuance.
  • The composite score is annual and backward-looking — it will not reflect an event from the last few months until World Bank data catches up; see the app's Live Conflicts tab for the qualitative, currently-relevant complement, and Upcoming Catalysts above for the forward-looking one.
  • All scores are relative rankings within this 34-country sample, not an absolute, globally-benchmarked index (see Coverage above).
  • Deeper qualitative fields — Financing Arrangements, Key Economic Partners, Trade/Sector Profiles, Major Economic Sanctions, and HDI — are deliberately scoped to 7 of the 34 countries (Turkey and the Horn of Africa group) with the strongest available public sourcing, rather than thinly covering all 34.
  • Historical context and conflict tracking are curated, hand-researched snapshots (fact-checked as of August 2026), not a live news feed.
  • Upcoming Catalysts covers 12 of the 34 countries by design — see Upcoming Catalysts above.
  • Risk indicators can identify vulnerability but cannot determine the timing or certainty of specific events.
  • Built with AI assistance, under the author's direction — both the code and the qualitative research were drafted with an AI assistant and fact-checked against the named sources shown throughout.
  • The monitor is not investment advice.

14. Additional Sources Used

The 11-factor risk score is built primarily on World Bank data, with IMF World Economic Outlook debt figures used as an automatic fallback. The qualitative layers — Historical Context, Live Conflicts, Key Economic Partners, Trade/Sector Profiles, and Credit Ratings — draw on a much broader set of reputable sources, reflecting how real political risk research actually works: no single database covers conflict dynamics, trade relationships, credit ratings, and fiscal data at once.
